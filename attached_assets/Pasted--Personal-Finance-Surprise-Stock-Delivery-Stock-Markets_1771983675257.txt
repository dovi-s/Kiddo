How can I give stock to a friend as a surprise gift?

Giving stock as a surprise gift is thoughtful and can build long-term value. Below is a clear, practical roadmap covering legal, tax, logistical, and surprise-preservation steps.

1) Choose how to transfer

Transfer directly into the recipient’s brokerage account: simplest if you know their account details (account number, brokerage name, DTC/ACAT transfer instructions). Transfer via an ACAT or broker-assisted transfer. Maintains recipient control and tax basis goes to donor (see tax point).
Gift shares using a custodial account for minors: open a custodial (UGMA/UTMA) account in the child’s name; the custodian (you or recipient if adult) manages until majority.
Transfer by delivering a physical stock certificate: rare and slower; requires endorsement and transfer form to the brokerage.
Use a donor-advised mechanism: donate appreciated stock to a donor-advised fund (DAF) in the recipient’s name if you want a charitable-style gift (different outcome).
Use fractional-share gifting services: some brokerages allow buying and transferring fractional shares or creating “stock gift” cards that the recipient redeems.
2) Verify legal and broker requirements

Confirm the recipient has a brokerage account that accepts transfers. If not, you may hold shares in your account and transfer later or use a brokerage that issues gift codes.
For securities transfer you’ll usually need: stock name/ticker, CUSIP, number of shares, donor investor account details, recipient account details, and a Medallion Signature Guarantee for certificate transfers or certain transfers.
International constraints: cross-border transfers can be complex; some brokers restrict transfers between countries.
3) Tax and cost considerations

Gift tax: in the U.S., 2025 annual exclusion is $18,000 per recipient (indexed annually). Gifts above that reduce lifetime exclusion or require filing IRS Form 709. Donor typically pays any gift tax, not recipient.
Donor’s cost basis: recipient inherits donor’s cost basis and holding period for capital gains tax purposes. This matters when the stock is sold.
Capital gains: if donor’s basis > current value, tax rules differ when done within three years before donor’s death—consult an advisor for estate planning implications.
Brokerage fees: some brokers charge transfer fees; check both brokers’ fee schedules.
For non-U.S. taxpayers, follow local gift and securities transfer tax rules.
4) Preserve the surprise

If you don’t know the recipient’s brokerage details:
Use a stock-gift card service or brokerage “gift” feature: you buy shares and the recipient receives an email/code to open an account and accept the gift.
Purchase a single share and place it in a new brokerage account you open in their name (with their info) only if you have legal authority—otherwise open a custodial account for a minor or use a gift card approach for adults.
Coordinate with the recipient’s email or documents subtly if the broker will notify them. Choose services that let you control timing of notification.
Physical presentation: print a brokerage confirmation, write a personalized certificate, or present a framed screenshot of the purchase (if preserving secrecy until reveal).
5) Step-by-step practical flow (common, low-friction method)
1. Decide stock and amount (whole or fractional shares).
2. Check if your broker supports gifting/transfers or offers a gift-code feature.
3. If using transfers: collect recipient brokerage details (account number & broker) discreetly OR arrange a timed redemption method.
4. Initiate transfer with your broker; follow transfer forms and provide any required guarantees.
5. Track completion; confirm acceptance at recipient’s broker.
6. Reveal the gift in person, by card, or with a printed confirmation.

6) Alternatives when secrecy or logistics block a direct transfer

Gift card / e-gift certificate from a brokerage that issues stocks (e.g., “stock gift” platforms).
Buy a share through a retail investing app, screenshot or print the holding, and give that as a symbolic gift; later transfer real shares when logistics allow.
Give cash earmarked for specific stock with a note and instructions; include suggested steps for buying it later.
7) Practical tips and pitfalls

Avoid opening an account in someone else’s name without permission (legal/identity issues).
Ensure the recipient will be comfortable receiving stock (liquidity, taxes).
If gifting appreciated securities, gifting is often more tax-efficient than selling and gifting cash—donor avoids capital gains and recipient retains donor basis (consult a tax advisor).
For sizable gifts, consult a tax or estate attorney to manage gift tax filings and long-term implications.
Examples (typical stories)

Example 1: A friend used their brokerage’s “gift a stock” feature—bought fractional shares, generated a redeemable link, then sent the link as a surprise e-card. Recipient clicked, created an account, and accepted the shares.
Example 2: Giver transferred full shares from their brokerage into the recipient’s existing account using ACAT; both brokers required simple transfer forms and the move completed in 3–7 business days.
Example 3: For a minor, a parent opened a custodial account and transferred stock into it, then presented a certificate-style printout at a birthday.
Final checklist before gifting

Confirm recipient’s broker can accept transfers or use a gift-card/ code option.
Verify tax and gift-limit implications.
Prepare transfer paperwork and any signature guarantees.
Plan the reveal method that preserves the surprise and communicates any necessary steps the recipient must take after receiving the gift.
If the gift is substantial, involve a tax or estate professional to avoid unintended tax consequences and to structure the transfer cleanly.

Certainly, it's done all the time. Normally they would have to establish a brokerage or dividend reinvestment account in their own name and then the shares can be transferred to that account, though you could also ask to have a stock certificate sent to you instead.
