Yeah - I agree w Tim. It depends on what the unrealized cap gains are on the fund(s) are. 2 key pieces of info that I would want for each fund are unrealized cap gains and expense ratios. Based on those, and what the funds actually are, I would make a determination if I should sell each one or not - if the ERs are low and unrealized cap gains are high, and they seem like reasonable funds, then you could consider keeping them as a "legacy fund". Maybe the ER is high, unrealized cap gains are high, and the fund is not what you want - if giving to charity is part of your plan, then donating these may be considered, as Tim says. Or if there is minimal cap gains or cap loss, you can just sell and then buy what you want (vtsax). But I would eval each one individually and decide what to do...

Johanna might explain it better but if I recollect it right, if a person dies and passes the stocks to you in his will, you get a step up in basis. So if the stock was bought for $30/share and is now $100, you get to use $100 as the new basis. If you sell it at $110, you get to pay taxes on only $10 in gain.

On the other hand, if the person is living while he gifted the stock and you do the same thing as noted above, you pay taxes on the difference between the original $30 and the new $110 per share. Even if you sell it right away you will pay capital gain taxes on the difference between $30 and $100.

This is the most important difference.

PS: Shares held for < 1 year pay a higher rate of cap gains taxes called short term CG taxes. Those held for >1 year pay a lower rate, called long term CG taxes.

If the stock were gifted (not inherited), the basis and holding period are the same as that of the person that gifted the stock to you. If these have very low cost basis (significant unrealized capital gains) and are good blue chip companies, I would hold them but not have dividends reinvested. If, on the other hand, there is minimal unrealized capital gains, then sell away. Of course, if they have been purchased within the last year and would be short term gains, I would at least wait until they had been owned (from date of the gifter's purchase) 1 day greater than 1 year so any gains were taxed at favorable long term capital gains rates (mostly 15%, depending on other income).
