1) The Elena Verna pricing mindset for this product
Non-negotiables

Two-sided marketplace pricing must be legible

Giver needs a fast checkout with minimal “fees confusion.”

Host (parent/recipient) needs predictable costs and a way to remove friction for guests.

Free must work as a growth engine without lighting money on fire

Free can be “cheap to serve” and still profitable on contribution volume, but only if you design fees correctly and control high-cost behaviors.

Hosts pay for premium, guests pay for convenience

Guests will tolerate a small fee if it feels like ticketing/registry.

Hosts will pay to remove that fee and make the experience feel premium.

You monetize on three time horizons

Transaction margin now (gift volume).

Subscription margin soon (repeat events, household).

AUM-like economics later (assets that stay invested).

2) What she would ship as the “final” pricing model
Core concept: split “fees” into two buckets users understand

Payment processing (card/Apple Pay rails)

Kora service fee (what you earn for the product)

Recommended default pricing
Free (default)

Guest pays: Service fee = 1.5% (capped at $10) + pass-through processing

Why this works: You monetize every gift. Hosts can start instantly. Guests understand “a small fee to send a gift.”

“Let guests pay fees” (default)

“I will cover fees” (host pays the service fee and processing for guests)

3) Cost stack you must account for (the “be real” part)

Your main cost categories:

A) Payment costs (every gift)

Card/Apple Pay processing (via Stripe or a PSP)

ACH fees (lower, but more ops)

Chargebacks, disputes, fraud tooling

B) Brokerage stack costs

Depends heavily on partner and configuration, but you will typically have:

Account opening and KYC/AML workflow costs

Clearing/custody and reporting obligations

Statement delivery and tax docs

Money movement rails

Support burdens

C) Product and ops costs that scale with volume

Customer support (especially around failed payments, KYC issues, refunds)

Identity verification vendor

Messaging/email/SMS and card generation

Security and compliance program (policies, audits, vendor management)

Insurance (E&O, cyber, potentially fidelity bond depending on setup)

D) Investment product costs

If you offer baskets/models:

You must be careful not to cross into personalized advice without the right structure

Generic baskets are simpler; personalization increases regulatory load

Takeaway: Your service fee has to cover more than Stripe. It covers the entire operational truth of “this gift became an investment.”

Is it weird to “pay so you can pay the fees”?

It can feel weird if you frame it that way. So you do not frame it that way.

You frame it as:

“Make gifts fee-free for guests”

“Upgrade your event experience”

“Cover fees for your guests + premium tools”

That is not weird. It maps to what people already understand:

Wedding registries and ticketing: someone pays so guests have a smoother experience

Fundraisers: “cover fees” toggle is common

Events: hosts routinely pay for convenience and polish

The important nuance: give hosts a toggle, not a moral decision

In Free, you can offer two options:

Guests pay fees (default)

Host covers fees (one-click, but it costs you)

If host covers fees, that becomes either:

A one-time upgrade (“Fee-free mode: $49”), or

A per-event add-on (“Cover fees for this event”), or

Included in Plus/Family

This feels natural because the host is not “paying to pay fees.” They are paying to remove friction and make the event feel premium.

What not to do

Do not make “host covers fees” available for free at scale. That will blow you up.

Do not use a low monthly price as the main plan. It under-monetizes your highest-intent moment and confuses people.

Free: $0 to create. Guests pay a small service fee (your revenue) plus processing.

Plus (per event): “Fee-free gifts + premium event tools.”

Family (annual): “Multiple kids and events, recurring gifting, fee-free pool.”
