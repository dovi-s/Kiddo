If you search for the downsides of custodial accounts, this is the one that comes up first, and it is real: a UTMA can reduce need-based financial aid more than other ways of saving for the same child.

We think you should hear that from us, with the actual math, rather than discover it later.

## How the aid formula counts assets

The FAFSA turns your family's finances into a number called the Student Aid Index, which colleges use to size need-based aid. Two facts drive everything here:

- Assets owned by the **student** are assessed at up to 20 percent.
- Assets owned by the **parent** are assessed at a maximum of about 5.64 percent.

A UTMA legally belongs to the child from the first dollar. That is its whole point, and it is also why the formula treats it as a student asset.

## The worked example

Take $10,000 saved for the same child, two ways:

- In a UTMA, the formula can count up to $2,000 against aid eligibility.
- In a parent-owned 529, it counts at most about $564.

Same money, same kid, roughly $1,400 of difference in the formula. Repeat that across larger balances and the gap grows with it.

## Who this actually matters for

The honest follow-up question is whether your family expects to qualify for need-based aid at all.

- If your income and assets place you above the need-based range at the schools your child is likely to attend, the assessment difference may change nothing in practice.
- If you expect to qualify, the difference is real and worth planning around, ideally before the FAFSA filing years.

The FAFSA also looks at assets as of the day you file, not at every dollar that ever passed through an account. Families in the aid window often talk through timing with an advisor.

## What families in the aid window do about it

Three approaches come up most often. All of them have tradeoffs, which is why this is advisor territory rather than a checklist:

1. **Spend the UTMA for the child's benefit before filing years.** Custodial money can pay for things that directly benefit the child, like a laptop, tutoring, or a first car. Money spent for the child before the FAFSA is filed is not assessed.
2. **Convert the UTMA into a custodial 529.** A 529 owned this way is reported as a parent asset on the FAFSA, which moves the assessment from up to 20 percent down to about 5.64 percent. The conversion requires selling the investments first, which can create capital gains tax for the child.
3. **Accept the math knowingly.** For many families the UTMA holds money that simply would not exist otherwise, because it arrived as birthday gifts from grandparents and godparents, fifty dollars at a time. Aid formulas measure savings. They do not measure whether the savings would have happened at all.

That third point is worth sitting with. A dollar that was never going to be saved cannot lose you aid. A grandparent's gift that became an investment instead of a toy is new money in your child's life, even after the formula takes its view of it.

## The practical rule

- Expecting need-based aid, and saving primarily for college from your own income? A parent-owned 529 is the cleaner instrument, and we say so plainly in our [UTMA vs 529 comparison](/blog/utma-vs-529-for-family-gifting).
- Building flexible, gift-driven savings your child controls as an adult? That is what a UTMA is for, and the aid treatment is the known price of that flexibility.
- In the aid window with an existing UTMA? Talk to a financial aid advisor before the filing years. The timing options above are most useful early.

Aid formulas change, and state rules vary. For current figures, check the federal student aid site directly, and treat this article as a map rather than advice for your specific situation.
